— What is XPS?

X-ray Photoelectron
Spectroscopy

Imagine being able to read the chemical identity of every atom sitting on the surface of a material — within just the outermost 5 to 10 nanometres. That is what XPS does.

A beam of X-rays is directed at a solid surface. The energy of those X-rays knocks electrons — called photoelectrons — out of the atoms. By measuring the kinetic energy of these emitted electrons, we can calculate the binding energy that held them in place. Since every element has a unique set of binding energies, XPS acts as a fingerprint reader for the periodic table — identifying not just which elements are present, but what chemical state they are in: whether copper is Cu⁰, Cu⁺, or Cu²⁺, for example.

xpsfitting.com — Biesinger Reference Pages ↗
🔬

The Photoelectric Effect

When an X-ray photon (typically Al Kα at 1486.7 eV) strikes an atom, a core-level electron is ejected. The binding energy is calculated as: Eb = hν − Ek − φ, where hν is the photon energy, Ek is the measured kinetic energy, and φ is the spectrometer work function.

📐

Surface Sensitivity

XPS probes only the outermost 5–10 nm of a material — roughly 3–10 atomic layers. This extreme surface sensitivity makes it invaluable for studying coatings, catalysts, and nanostructured materials where surface chemistry governs performance.

📊

Chemical State & Quantification

The position of an XPS peak shifts depending on the oxidation state and bonding environment — the chemical shift. Careful peak fitting following Biesinger's reference procedures allows quantitative determination of elemental composition and relative proportions of chemical species.

⚗️

Applications

XPS is used across materials science, catalysis, corrosion science, thin-film chemistry, and energy materials. In my research, it is the primary tool for characterizing Cu₂O nanostructured surfaces and understanding how their chemical state drives photocatalytic activity.
